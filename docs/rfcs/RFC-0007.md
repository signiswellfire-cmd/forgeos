# Abstract

This RFC defines the Decision Authority Matrix (DAM) used by ForgeOS.

The Decision Authority Matrix establishes **who has authority to make which decisions**, **who provides expertise**, **who must be consulted**, and **who must be informed**.

The objective is to prevent organizational ambiguity while preserving engineering autonomy and Founder control over strategic direction.

The Decision Authority Matrix is a governance mechanism—not a permission system.

---

# Motivation

As engineering organizations grow, ambiguity surrounding decision ownership becomes increasingly expensive.

Typical failure modes include:

* multiple people making conflicting decisions,
* unnecessary Founder involvement,
* engineering paralysis,
* hidden ownership,
* duplicated approvals,
* slow execution.

ForgeOS requires explicit authority boundaries.

---

# Problem Statement

Organizations frequently confuse:

* responsibility,
* expertise,
* execution,
* ownership,
* approval.

These concepts are related but not identical.

Without a formal authority model:

* decisions become inconsistent,
* governance weakens,
* accountability disappears,
* organizational learning deteriorates.

---

# Goals

The Decision Authority Matrix shall:

* define decision ownership;
* separate authority from expertise;
* reduce Founder cognitive load;
* preserve engineering autonomy;
* support explainable governance;
* integrate with Executive Meetings.

---

# Non-Goals

This RFC does not define:

* authentication,
* security permissions,
* user roles,
* operating system authorization,
* implementation access control.

Those concerns belong to implementation.

---

# Decision

ForgeOS adopts a **Decision Authority Matrix** in which every significant decision has one clearly identified decision owner.

Consultation may involve many Professionals.

Authority belongs to exactly one organizational role unless explicitly delegated.

---

# Decision Roles

Each decision identifies the following governance roles.

## Decision Owner

The Professional with final authority for the decision.

Exactly one owner shall exist.

---

## Contributors

Professionals who provide expertise.

Contributors influence decisions but do not approve them.

---

## Reviewers

Professionals responsible for validating quality, consistency, or compliance.

Review does not imply authority.

---

## Stakeholders

Professionals affected by the decision.

Stakeholders should remain informed but do not necessarily participate.

---

# Authority Principles

## Single Decision Owner

Every significant decision shall have one owner.

Shared ownership creates ambiguity.

---

## Expertise Before Hierarchy

Recommendations should be driven by expertise.

Approval follows organizational governance.

---

## Delegation

Authority may be delegated.

Responsibility remains traceable.

Delegation should be explicit and documented.

---

## Traceability

Every approved decision records:

* owner,
* contributors,
* reviewers,
* supporting knowledge,
* approval date,
* rationale.

Future contributors should understand not only the decision but also why the organization trusted that decision.

---

# Authority Levels

ForgeOS recognizes five conceptual authority levels.

## Level 1 — Founder

Authority over:

* vision,
* business strategy,
* licensing,
* market positioning,
* public product direction.

---

## Level 2 — Executive

Authority over:

* organizational architecture,
* strategic engineering,
* organizational capability,
* governance,
* engineering standards.

---

## Level 3 — Professional

Authority over:

* discipline-specific decisions,
* implementation planning,
* technical recommendations,
* operational execution.

---

## Level 4 — Team

Authority over:

* mission execution,
* coordination,
* task prioritization,
* delivery planning.

---

## Level 5 — Mission

Authority over:

* temporary execution activities,
* implementation details,
* operational work required to complete a Mission.

Mission authority expires upon Mission completion.

# Decision Categories

The matrix classifies decisions by category.

Examples include:

## Strategic

Examples:

* product vision,
* licensing,
* platform direction.

Default owner:

Founder.

---

## Architectural

Examples:

* RFC approval,
* platform architecture,
* engineering standards.

Default owner:

Chief Software Architect.

---

## Organizational

Examples:

* professional responsibilities,
* organizational processes,
* capability development.

Default owner:

Chief Executive Officer.

---

## Technical

Examples:

* implementation approach,
* refactoring,
* optimization,
* testing.

Default owner:

Responsible Professional.

---

## Operational

Examples:

* deployments,
* releases,
* monitoring,
* incident response.

Default owner:

Responsible operational Professional.

---

# Decision Lifecycle

Every decision progresses through:

1. Proposal.
2. Context collection.
3. Executive discussion (when required).
4. Recommendation.
5. Approval.
6. Recording.
7. Implementation.
8. Knowledge promotion.

Authority applies only at the approval stage.

---

# Executive Meeting Integration

Executive Meetings generate recommendations.

The Decision Authority Matrix determines who may approve those recommendations.

Meetings inform authority.

They do not replace it.

---

# Knowledge Integration

Every approved decision becomes:

* a Knowledge Object,
* a graph node,
* an organizational asset,
* a traceable historical record.

Decision authority therefore contributes directly to organizational memory.

---

# Alternatives Considered

## Consensus-Based Governance

Rejected because consensus often obscures accountability.

---

## Founder Approval for Everything

Rejected because it creates organizational bottlenecks and increases Founder cognitive load.

---

## AI Autonomous Authority

Rejected because AI execution should remain accountable to organizational governance.

---

# Consequences

Positive outcomes include:

* explicit accountability,
* predictable governance,
* explainable decision history,
* improved organizational scalability,
* reduced approval ambiguity.

Trade-offs include:

* governance documentation,
* explicit authority management,
* additional organizational modeling.

These trade-offs are accepted because they strengthen long-term engineering capability.
